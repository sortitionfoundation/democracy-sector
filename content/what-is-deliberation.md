Deliberative public engagement brings a group of people together - through structured dialogue and debate - to learn about an issue, weigh different perspectives and trade-offs, and come to a shared way forward that informs better decision making. At its heart, deliberation is a convergent process - participants move from individual starting positions towards a collective view, set of recommendations, or agreed actions.

The word 'deliberation' means 'to weigh up'. Deliberative public engagement brings a diverse group of people together to learn about an issue, hear from a range of perspectives, weigh up options and trade-offs, and reach a shared view that informs a real decision. It is a form of collective reasoning. Participants do not simply state their views - they work out together what should happen, and why.

Done well, deliberation helps us move from public opinion - our top-of-the-head perspectives - to public judgement, considered views that have been tested against evidence, other people's experiences and the trade-offs involved. That is what makes it so valuable to decision makers facing complex, contested questions. It is also why deliberation has become an increasingly prominent part of UK democracy over the past decade, from council chambers to central government.

This page sets out what deliberation is (and is not), how it differs from dialogue, and some of the UK processes that demonstrate what it can achieve.

## Key features of deliberation

Deliberation can take many forms - citizens' assemblies, juries and panels, in person, online or hybrid. What they share is a set of core ingredients. A deliberative process brings together:

- **A clear purpose, linked to a real decision.** Deliberation starts with a genuine question that a decision maker is ready to act on - a policy, plan, service or budget - rather than a conversation for its own sake.
- **A diverse group of people.** Participants are recruited to reflect the wider population, or because they bring direct experience of the issue. This diversity is essential - mixed groups tend to make better decisions than like-minded ones.
- **Balanced information and evidence.** Participants hear from a range of experts, stakeholders and people with lived experience, and can question what they hear. This ensures that conclusions are shaped by evidence and multiple perspectives, not by whoever controls the information.
- **Time to learn, reflect and reconsider.** Deliberation is a journey, not a snapshot. Sessions build on each other so people can learn, test ideas and arrive at a considered view - which may or may not be where they started.
- **Impartial facilitation.** Skilled, independent facilitators ensure that everyone can contribute on an equal footing, that no group dominates, and that the quieter voices are heard as clearly as the loudest. This maintains a separation between process and content.
- **Reasoned, collective conclusions.** Participants weigh options, give reasons for their positions and work towards a shared outcome - something they all support, or can at least live with. This convergence, from individual starting points to a collective view, is what sets deliberation apart from dialogue.

## What deliberation is not

Deliberation is sometimes confused with other sorts of engagement. Being clear about what it is not helps protect what makes it distinctive. Deliberation is not:

- **A debate.** Debate is about winning the argument. Deliberation is about weighing options together and finding where common ground lies.
- **Persuasion.** A process designed to steer people towards an answer that has already been decided is not deliberation - it's a public relations or tick box exercise.
- **An opinion poll or focus group.** Polls and focus groups capture what people think now. Deliberation gives people the time, information and space to reach a considered judgement together.
- **Consultation or research by another name.** Using deliberative methods purely to gather insight, without a clear link to a decision, transparency or any sharing of power, may still be valuable, but it is not deliberative engagement for decision making.
- **Public dialogue on its own.** Dialogue opens up an issue and maps different views. Deliberation goes further and moves towards a shared conclusion (see below).
- **A replacement for representative democracy.** Deliberative processes do not take decisions away from elected representatives. They complement and strengthen them, giving decision makers a clear, considered picture of what an informed public thinks, and why.

The deliberative standards draw this line explicitly. If a process does not meet the Core tier of the [standards](/framework/), it may still be worthwhile, but it should not be described as deliberative engagement.

## Dialogue and deliberation - what is the difference?

Dialogue and deliberation are close relatives, and the terms are often used interchangeably. Both are forms of participatory engagement, and both depend on good facilitation, listening and respect for different views. The difference lies in where they are heading.

**Dialogue opens things up.** It is an exploratory, discovery-oriented conversation - people share experiences, listen, reflect and build understanding of each other's perspectives. It widens the range of possibilities and does not need to reach a conclusion.

**Deliberation narrows things down.** It starts with dialogue - which is a prerequisite for deliberation - but then moves on to weighing options, working through trade-offs and reaching a shared conclusion that can inform a decision.

|  | Dialogue | Deliberation |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Build understanding of an issue and of each other | Reach a shared view, conclusion or recommendations on a question |
| **Direction** | Divergent - opens up possibilities | Convergent - narrows towards a conclusion |
| **End point** | Can stay open-ended | Agreement where possible, or something everyone can live with |
| **Link to decisions** | Can inform thinking at any stage | Tied to a specific decision someone is ready to act on |
| **Common formats** | Conversation cafés, World Café, public meetings, distributed dialogue | Citizens' assemblies, citizens' juries, deliberative panels and workshops |

In practice, the two often sit side by side. Dialogue may form one or more stages within a deliberative process, or it can help to shape the questions that a deliberative process goes on to answer.

Public dialogue has a strong track record of its own in the UK. Through [Sciencewise](https://sciencewise.org.uk/), the UK government has used public dialogue for more than two decades to involve people in decisions about science and technology. Sciencewise has a publicly available set of [guiding principles](https://sciencewise.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Guiding-Principles.pdf) and a [quality framework](https://sciencewise.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/Quality-in-Public-Dialogue.pdf) for public dialogue.

## The double diamond - a model for deliberation

A helpful way to picture how a deliberative process flows is the double diamond. Developed by the Design Council in 2005 to describe the design process, it maps neatly onto deliberation - two rounds of opening up (divergent thinking) followed by narrowing down (convergent thinking).

![Double diamond diagram: Discover (learn and share) and Define (agree priorities) form the first diamond, understand the issue; Develop (explore options) and Deliver (reach agreement) form the second, shape the answer.](/files/double-diamond.png)

### The first diamond - understanding the issue

- **Discover:** Participants open up the issue. They hear balanced evidence from experts, stakeholders and people with lived experience, share their own experiences and listen to each other. This stage is largely dialogue.
- **Define:** Participants start to narrow down - drawing out what matters most to them, the values and principles that should guide a decision, and the priorities they want to focus on.

### The second diamond - shaping the answer

- **Develop:** With a shared understanding in place, participants open up again - generating and exploring a range of options, testing ideas and working through the trade-offs involved.
- **Deliver:** Finally, participants weigh up the options and converge on a shared outcome: recommendations they all support, or can at least live with, which are handed to the decision maker to respond to.

The model shows why deliberation needs time. Without the first diamond, people jump to solutions before they understand the problem. Without the second, there may be good conversation, but no conclusions that a decision maker can act on. It also shows where dialogue fits - as the divergent, opening-up stages within a wider process that works towards a conclusion.

## Deliberation in action

Over the past decade, deliberation has moved from the margins to the mainstream of UK democracy - across all four nations, and at every level from neighbourhood to Parliament. The examples below are among the processes that have shaped this shift.

They use a range of formats. Citizens' assemblies tend to be larger - often 50 to 100 or more people - meeting over several weekends. Citizens' juries are smaller groups, typically 12 to 24 people, who hear evidence and reach a verdict over a few days. Citizens' panels vary, from one-off groups to standing panels that meet over months or years.

### Citizens' assemblies

**[Citizens' Assembly for Northern Ireland](https://www.involve.org.uk/news-opinion/projects/full-report-citizens-assembly-northern-ireland) (2018).** Northern Ireland's first citizens' assembly met over two weekends to consider the future of social care for older people, producing resolutions on funding and the need for transformational change.

**[Climate Assembly UK](https://www.climateassembly.uk/report/read/about-climate-assembly-uk.html) (2020).** Commissioned by six House of Commons select committees, 108 people from every part of the UK spent six weekends answering the question: how should the UK meet its target of net zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050? Involve designed and facilitated the assembly, which published its recommendations in September 2020.

**[Scotland's Climate Assembly](https://www.gov.scot/publications/scottish-government-response-scotlands-climate-assembly-recommendations-action/pages/2/) (2020–22).** Grounded in Scotland's Climate Change Act 2019, more than 100 people met online over seven weekends and agreed 81 recommendations, laid before the Scottish Parliament in June 2021. Working with the Children's Parliament, it also brought children's voices into the process. The Scottish Government published a formal response to every recommendation.

**[Waltham Forest Citizens' Assembly on the Future of Neighbourhood Policing](https://www.walthamforest.gov.uk/news/citizens-assembly-action-plan-launched-address-crime-and-safety) (2024).** The UK's first citizens' assembly on neighbourhood policing, commissioned jointly by Waltham Forest Council and the Metropolitan Police. Fifty residents produced a vision and recommendations, and in May 2025 the council's Cabinet approved a joint police and council action plan in response.

### Citizens' juries

**[Citizens' juries on AI explainability](https://jefferson-center.org/citizens-juries-artificial-intelligence/) (2019).** Commissioned by the Information Commissioner's Office and the NIHR Greater Manchester Patient Safety Translational Research Centre, two juries of 18 people met for five days each in Coventry and Manchester. They weighed up whether AI systems should explain their decisions, even if that makes them less accurate. Their findings informed the ICO and the Alan Turing Institute's guidance on explaining decisions made with AI.

**[Kendal Climate Change Citizens' Jury](https://www.kendaltowncouncil.gov.uk/climate-jury/) (2020).** Following its climate emergency declaration, Kendal Town Council convened a citizens' jury, designed and facilitated by Shared Future. Twenty residents met over 11 sessions to answer the question: what should Kendal do about climate change? They agreed 27 recommendations. A recommendations panel chaired by the Town Council has since met to track progress, with many recommendations being taken forward through partnerships such as the Zero Carbon Cumbria Partnership.

**[Citizens' juries on health data sharing in a pandemic](https://arc-gm.nihr.ac.uk/media/Resources/ARC/Digital%20Health/Citizen%20Juries/12621_NIHR_Juries_Report_ELECTRONIC.pdf) (2021).** Three online juries - one drawn from across England, one from Greater Manchester and one from West and East Sussex - considered how patient data had been shared during Covid-19. Commissioned by NIHR ARC Greater Manchester, NHSX and the National Data Guardian for Health and Social Care, they backed the use of emergency data-sharing powers but called for much greater transparency.

### Citizens' panels

**[Scottish Parliament Citizens' Panel on Participation](https://www.parliament.scot/chamber-and-committees/committees/committee-reports/cppp/2023/9/12/cppps062023r02) (2022).** Nineteen randomly selected people met over two weekends at Holyrood to consider how the Parliament can make sure diverse voices and communities from all parts of Scotland influence its work. They agreed 17 recommendations, and the committee that commissioned them concluded that the Parliament should use citizens' panels more regularly.

**[Citizens' Panel on news and democracy in Wales](https://university.open.ac.uk/wales/sites/www.open.ac.uk.wales/files/files/IWA-OU_Citizens-Voice-Peoples-News_ENG.pdf) (2022).** The Institute of Welsh Affairs and The Open University in Wales brought together 15 people from across Wales, recruited by civic lottery, to ask: how well does the news help you understand politics? Their nine recommendations - on media regulation, citizenship education and recognising Welshness - were aimed at the Welsh Government and the Senedd.

**[NG Citizens](https://www.involve.org.uk/our-work/our-projects/what-if-national-gallery-was-shaped-nation) (2025 onwards).** The first national citizens' assembly held by a UK museum or gallery. Fifty people from across the four nations, selected by civic lottery, considered what they wanted from the National Gallery. Their principles will inform the Gallery's 10-year strategy, and from 2026 a citizens' panel of former members will help put them into practice, through to 2030.

## Doing deliberation well

Each of these processes worked because the fundamentals were in place - a clear mandate, the right people in the room, balanced information, skilled facilitation, enough time, and a commitment from decision makers to respond. As deliberation spreads into new places and new formats - online, hybrid and AI-assisted - holding on to those fundamentals matters more than ever.
