These standards exist to uphold the integrity of deliberative practice and protect what is distinctive and impactful about [deliberation](/what-is-deliberation/). They codify practice the sector already broadly shares, brought together and made explicit. They do not impose an external template or prescribe particular methods or technologies. Rather, they seek to provide guardrails and illustrate good practice, whether a process is in person, online, hybrid, AI-assisted, or an entirely new form. The goal is creativity and innovation within a framework that protects what sets deliberation apart from other forms of engagement.

Citizens' assemblies in the UK already have an [established set of standards](https://www.involve.org.uk/resources/knowledge-base/how-do-i-setup-citizens-assembly/standards-citizens-assemblies) - but deliberative practice is changing fast, with growing diversity in processes run at different scales, by different actors, using new technologies. Beyond citizens' assemblies, the sector does not yet have a formally agreed set of standards. Members of the UK's deliberative democracy sector have collaborated to close this gap, developing standards that apply across diverse and innovative forms of deliberative engagement for decision making. These build on existing frameworks including [Involve's Deliberative Principles](https://www.involve.org.uk/resources/knowledge-base/what-are-principles-effective-deliberative-public-engagement), [Sciencewise's Guiding Principles and Quality Framework](https://sciencewise.org.uk/about-sciencewise/our-guiding-principles/), [OECD Best Practice Principles](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_339306da-en/full-report/component-9.html) and [IAP2 Values](https://www.iap2.org/general/custom.asp?page=corevalues).

This framework represents the sector's collective good practice, and focuses specifically on deliberative engagement for decision making - processes such as citizens' assemblies, juries and panels, where a group is recruited to deliberate on a question, whether to reflect the wider population or because its members bring direct experience of the issue at hand. While these standards have been designed for deliberative processes that inform decision making and are commissioned by an institution with the power to act on the recommendations, many will also apply to, and be useful for, other types of processes such as self-governed or community-led processes.

## What this framework is not for

To keep the term 'deliberative engagement' meaningful, this framework excludes:

- **Deliberative research, polling or consultation** - work using deliberative methods purely for research or consultative purposes, without transparency requirements, public evaluation, or power sharing.
- **Learning for democracy and popular education** - community conversations, civic education and democratic education work.
- **Public dialogue** - surfacing and mapping different perspectives or lines of disagreement without moving towards a shared conclusion; this may form a stage within deliberative engagement.

These are all valuable in their own right but serve different purposes and do not require the same decision-linking infrastructure that these standards set out.

## A note on power

Process is never neutral. Who sets the question, who participates, whose knowledge counts, and who benefits are fundamentally questions of power. This framework can help ensure a process is well-designed, but it cannot guarantee that it is just. We are committed to ongoing reflection on this tension, and to governance that keeps the framework accountable to those it is meant to serve - not only those who commission and deliver deliberation.

This is a living document - we commit to revisiting and revising it after six months, then annually thereafter, as evidence emerges, new contexts arise, and the sector learns. We list the [contributors to these standards](/contributors/) for transparency.
